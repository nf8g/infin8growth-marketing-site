How to Find Your Business's Edge with AI

The best advice you're getting about AI might be technically correct and completely wrong for you.

By Marshall Tuten

Early in my first business, someone gave me a simple playbook for managing a big team. It was a good playbook. It came from someone who had done something like what I was shooting for so I decided to follow it.

Unfortunately in the end, while some results were effective, the strategy worked against me.

The advice wasn't all bad. But it was built for a different personality and business than the one I was actually building. I was pushing on process, testing the edges of my industry, trying to do things the original author didn't want to do or didn't value. Every time I followed his approach instead of my own read of the situation, I sanded down the exact thing that made my business mine. I did that to myself. And it took me a while to see it, because the advice was so clearly correct.

The most dangerous advice you'll ever get isn't the advice that's wrong. Wrong advice is easy to test. See if it fails, then drop it. The dangerous kind is the advice that's technically correct and completely wrong for your actual vision, culture, and long-term goals.

The numbers can seemingly work and it can still be the wrong move.

Getting handed the wrong game

AI has made it easier than ever to build a playbook or set of rules, systematize it, productize it, then sell it. Sometimes that's not the best way to approach building with AI. I've come to watch for three sources of this type of dynamic.

The first is the person operating one level up from you. Someone who's run a very similar business. Their advice is real but it's also shaped in a time and context that isn't necessarily yours. For example, someone protecting a portfolio has a completely different risk model than someone building a single business they actually run. They'll hand you their risk model and call it wisdom.

The second is the guru. Access to a clean framework, "five steps" to master {X}, "here's exactly what to do when {Y}". Frameworks are useful. But a framework is an average. It's built to be right on average, across a lot of businesses, which means it's almost never precisely right for yours, especially in the places where your advantage lives.

The third is someone a few steps ahead. This one's the sneakiest, because you trust them, and they mean well. But they're describing the path that worked for their business, their timing, their strengths. Follow it too closely and you inherit their constraints along with their tips.

None of these people are lying to you. That's the whole problem. Be careful whose model you inherit.

Why this is the most important thing to learn. Now.

For most of business history, borrowing someone else's exact playbook made sense. The gap between what you personally knew and what you could actually build was enormous. A borrowed process, hired expertise, a proven system were reliable because building your own version of everything was too slow and too expensive.

Borrowing playbooks still helps, but AI has made that gap MUCH thinner.

The distance between your own mind, your experience, your goals, and what you can actually put into the world has collapsed in a way I didn't expect to see this fast.

There are things you understand about your business that nobody else does. The specific ways you serve your customers and processes you've refined over years can now be taught to a tool and put to work directly. You don't have to translate it into someone else's system anymore.

Which is exactly why the loudest AI advice right now is the wrong game for most of the people hearing it.

The Wrong Frame

"AI will replace your team." You've heard this. And technically, you can point to cases where it's true. But for most business owners, it's the wrong frame entirely, and it makes people build the wrong thing.

Don't go looking for what to cut instead of what to strengthen.

Don't treat AI as a way to remove people instead of a way to make the people you have far more effective.

Don't chase the version of AI that fits a headline instead of the version that fits your business.

What it actually looks like when it fits

Let me give you a real example from our work.

A lot of our clients run ticketing systems. Their customers come to buy a service, an experience, a visit. And these systems get complicated. Buyers can't always tell which ticket is the right one for them. When that happens, two things break at once: the business loses the sale, and the customer walks away with a worse experience before they've even arrived.

This is both a technology and communication problem tangled together. So we use AI to untangle it.

Our goal is to break down the communication barrier at the moment of purchase, guide the customer to the option that actually fits what they came for, and do it in a way that feels natural. The customer buys the right thing and experience improves before they walk in the door. Our client's staff stop burning hours on the same repetitive back-and-forth. And the business gets data out of the whole interaction that tells them something useful about staffing and where to put their people.

Nobody got replaced. A team got freed up to do the work only humans should be doing, and the customers got a better experience. That's what AI working with and for a business looks like, in one specific business, solving one specific problem that came from that business's actual context.

The edge you already have

The roots of what we build at Infin8 Growth come from fundamental business problems:

  1. How do you move complex information between systems and people and timezones without errors piling up? How do you catch mistakes before they cost you?
  2. How do you rely on people without making yourself fragile when someone gets sick or leaves?
  3. How do you keep a process airtight and still leave room to improve it?

Some of those answers are the same across every company. But the answers that actually give you an advantage are almost always specific to your business. That's especially true where it matters most: how clients experience your service, how easy it is to buy, and how your team executes and interacts with your business knowledge.

The thing that makes your business worth working with is often the thing no generic playbook can describe.

Where to actually start

First, look at your own processes and your own people, because they already hold the secret to your next move.

The catch? Finding that move means facing the parts that don't work. You can't teach AI to strengthen your business until you've been honest about where it's actually weak. That's uncomfortable, and it's exactly where the edge is hiding. Ask AI to help you uncover those weaknesses.

Second, treat AI as a skill, not a purchase.

Nobody gets good at this by buying the right tool and walking away. It's closer to learning an instrument than flipping a switch. You practice, you get a feel for what it's good and bad at, you learn how to describe your business to it clearly.

Third, get a guide when you need one.

There's a difference between learning to use AI and building the right setup for your specific company: the right information feeding it, the right architecture connecting it to your tools and your knowledge, the right fit for how you actually operate. That's the part where a good guide saves you months of wrong turns. Not someone handing you their playbook, but someone helping you uncover yours.

Your edge is already there. Go find it.
